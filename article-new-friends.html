Meeting new people without hiding your ideas

Small, flexible ways to join conversations at university, clubs, and social events.

Fluent Minds educational guide · Updated 18 November 2026

Practical education and planning ideas, not individual treatment. These guides have not been clinically reviewed. A qualified speech-language professional can help you choose support that fits your needs.

Choose a setting you want to be in

Shared activities give a conversation somewhere to begin. A programming club, study group, or volunteering session lets you ask about the task in front of you. Choose an activity you care about, rather than attending only to test your speech.

Set a small participation goal: introduce yourself to one person, ask about a project, or share a useful link. The goal can change once you arrive. You are allowed to listen, leave early, or try another event.

Give yourself several ways into the group

You can ask a friend to introduce you, join a smaller conversation, or message the organiser beforehand. If names feel pressured, use a name badge or write your name while still choosing to speak if you wish.

Simple openings include “How did you get involved?” and “What are you working on?” Use questions you are actually interested in. You do not have to keep every conversation going or entertain everyone you meet.

Measure connection, not performance

Afterward, ask whether you learned something about someone or shared something that mattered to you. A conversation with stuttering can still be warm, funny, and interesting. You can send a follow-up message about the shared activity.

If a group repeatedly rushes or mocks you, the problem deserves attention. Speak with the organiser or choose a more welcoming space. You do not need to earn basic respect by speaking differently. Consider peer support if you would like to meet others who understand the experience of stuttering.
