Confidence

A classroom checklist for teachers

Agree on participation, turn-taking, and assessment with the student who stutters.

Fluent Minds educational guide · Updated 18 November 2026

Practical education and planning ideas, not individual treatment. These guides have not been clinically reviewed. A qualified speech-language professional can help you choose support that fits your needs.

Ask privately instead of guessing

Invite the student to explain what helps them participate. You might ask about answering the register, reading aloud, group work, and presentations. Do not assume that a quiet student wants to avoid every speaking task or that a confident student needs no support.

Agree on how you will revisit the plan. A student may prefer one arrangement in a small seminar and another in a large lecture. Check before discussing their stutter with classmates.

Make discussion easier to enter

  • Use clear turn-taking and discourage interruptions.
  • Allow thinking and speaking time.
  • Offer ways to contribute through speech, writing, or a diagram when the learning objective allows.
  • Discuss reading order and presentation format beforehand.

When marking an answer, separate subject understanding from assumptions about fluency. Ask your institution how to document and approve any formal assessment arrangement. The examples here are teaching suggestions, not a description of Bangladeshi legal requirements.

Respond to reactions in the room

Address mocking or imitation promptly through the school’s procedures. Check privately with the student about what they need, rather than asking them to teach the whole class in the moment.

Review what is working after a real lesson. “Did you have enough time to explain your answer?” is more useful than “Was your speech good today?” Collaboration with the student, family where appropriate, and their clinician can help keep support practical and consistent.
