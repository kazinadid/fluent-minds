How to read a stuttering treatment claim

A practical checklist for separating an interesting finding from a promise that applies to everyone.

Fluent Minds educational guide · Updated 18 November 2026

Practical education and planning ideas, not individual treatment. These guides have not been clinically reviewed. A qualified speech-language professional can help you choose support that fits your needs.

Find the exact claim

Copy the headline into your notes, then look for the original study or clinical guidance it cites. “Participants improved on a measure” is a different claim from “this cures everyone.” Check whether the page is describing a study, selling a service, or sharing one person’s experience.

A testimonial can tell you what someone valued. It cannot, by itself, establish what will happen for another person. Look for a clear distinction between the two.

Ask how the result was obtained

  • Who took part, and how many people were included?
  • Were their ages and circumstances similar to yours?
  • Was there a comparison group?
  • What changed: speech frequency, effort, participation, or wellbeing?
  • Were outcomes checked after the programme ended?
  • Were limitations and possible conflicts of interest disclosed?

You do not need advanced statistics to ask these questions. If an article omits the details, follow its references or ask the provider to explain.

Use evidence in a personal discussion

Write down the outcome you care about and bring the claim to a qualified clinician. Ask what is known, what is uncertain, and how they would assess whether an approach is useful for you.

ASHA’s clinical practice portal is a professional starting point with references, not a personalised prescription. A responsible support conversation should leave room for your preferences and for uncertainty. You can take time to decide before committing money or agreeing to a programme.
